Chainy Game Design Document​

Gameplay First Edition​

Version 2.0 • Adopted October 23, 2026 • Canonical game design

An offline island-restoration adventure for learning Web3, Blockchain and AI.

The player helps residents restore island services, uses a fictional digital economy, repairs a damaged local blockchain record, and teaches and checks an AI helper. Learning happens through consequential actions; short quizzes and vocabulary activities support those actions.

November 10, 2026 — Cryptita Plays community beta testing: deliver one complete Web3 restoration quest and one Blockchain repair challenge with dependable local saving. Add one AI training-and-test activity only after these pass Android checks. A broader three-world introduction is a stretch target, not a promise of 50 percent completion.

The owner reports that movement and dialogue are currently playable. The lead developer reports availability of up to 16–18 hours per day from October 23 through November 21. Neither statement is an independently tested build result. Estimates in Section 7 use focused implementation hours, not all available waking hours.

Adopted by the project owner on October 23, 2026, including the standalone Wordle and BlockMatch deferrals. This is the canonical GDD. Sherwin owns design decisions, Christian technical validation, Sean authored project documentation, Luis production art and Miguel UI implementation. Adoption specifies intended behavior; it does not establish implemented or tested behavior.

The beta is Cryptita Plays community beta testing on November 10, 2026; the former Marinduque school event is superseded. Pre-final remains November 21, 2026. Community testing must not be described as approved school/pilot-area research without the necessary confirmation. See the baseline, acceptance checklist, task plan, and GDD directory and change log. Earlier sources are preserved in the adoption archive.

All illustrated game screens are design concepts, not screenshots of a playable build. Generated artwork is not an import-ready Unity asset kit.

This edition follows the supplied Games Design Document — Project Vanishing as its main structural reference: brief, gameplay and controls, world and locations, art and GUI development, storyboard and sample narrative, sound, systems, characters, production plan and sources. Chainy’s own learning requirements replace that example’s combat, weapon progression, reputation and PC controls.

Reading the scope labels​

CORE means the adopted November 10 delivery boundary, subject to the first Android checkpoint. STRETCH means work admitted only after the core passes. FULL means retained in the full experience but not promised for November 10. LATER SERVICE means teacher provisioning, collection or distribution work deferred behind gameplay.

These labels are delivery boundaries, not evidence that a feature is complete. The school’s completion formula is unknown. No task count, illustration, estimated hour or chapter count establishes its required percentage.

Main changes from the previous proposal​

  • Keep the three-world journey, named guides and twelve hands-on concepts.
  • Put the playable restoration loop ahead of the teacher-service platform.
  • Keep local saving in the core: it is essential gameplay, not optional analytics work.
  • Replace a broad pre-beta wish list with explicit gates, recovery rules and fallback scope.
  • Preserve the full AI idea, but sequence the small example-based classifier before the assistant and agent exercises.
  • Separate the Blockchain repair exercise from the existing BlockMatch mini-game. They are not interchangeable names or implementations.

1 Game objectives and brief​

1.1 Game brief​

Chainy is a single-player, offline Unity/C# Android learning adventure for learners aged 12–17. It uses a fixed orthographic isometric view, compact low-poly environments, touchscreen movement and contextual interaction. English is the first language. No real cryptocurrency, real wallet, public blockchain, financial account or cloud AI service is required.

The intended player experience is curiosity followed by useful action: “I need to restore the workshop; I can earn a reward, buy a tool, check the ownership record and use the tool.” Definitions explain an action the learner has just encountered instead of becoming the whole activity.

One personal phone per learner is the intended school model. Once enrolled in the full product, play and saving do not depend on the teacher remaining nearby. The November gameplay build uses a clearly identified local demo profile until the later enrollment service exists. It must not present an unimplemented voucher screen as a working security boundary.

1.2 Narrative brief​

Island services have stopped working. The workshop needs light and supplies; the beacon’s local record display has been corrupted; the assistance center gives unreliable advice. Residents ask the Explorer for practical help.

Sean introduces how to use the island’s digital economy. Matthew helps reconstruct verified records at the beacon. Lee helps train and evaluate an advisory bot. Each restoration visibly changes the world: a workshop lights up, a beacon shines, then a dispatch center resumes supervised service.

This is a helpful adventure, not a combat game. The motivation is restoration and responsibility, not trading profit, punishment for mistakes or competition over wealth.

1.3 Learning objectives​

WorldLearner should be able to doEvidence in play
Web3Distinguish access, ownership and payment; explain a fee; use a conditional programReview a purchase, authorize it, inspect its record and use owned equipment
BlockchainDistinguish linked records, validation and agreement; identify stale or altered dataRepair a local sequence from a trusted reference and explain a mismatch
AIExplain examples, predictions and testing; recognize limits; verify adviceChange training examples, test fresh requests and require evidence before action

Completion is not automatically mastery. A correct answer after a hint is useful practice, but different evidence from an unassisted first response or a fresh transfer question.

1.4 Design pillars and exclusions​

Every required concept should support a meaningful player choice, visible system response and understandable explanation. A term may be a tool, station, action, cost or record; not everything should become collectible loot.

Keep journeys short and landmarks readable. Reuse one interaction grammar across worlds: inspect, decide, confirm, see the result and save. Let mistakes invite explanation and retry without permanently losing essential resources.

Excluded from this version: multiplayer, real payments, speculation, live exchanges, public-chain deployment, real seed phrases, live generative chat, procedural open worlds, combat, skill trees, complex NPC schedules and mandatory online student accounts.

2 Gameplay​

2.1 Overview​

Full-product flow: first-time Teacher or Student selection → authorized setup → Play → world selection → exploration → guide or resident → quest action → feedback → saved checkpoint.

Gameplay-milestone flow: local demo profile → Play → world selection → the same offline quest loop. The temporary profile route is explicitly a demonstration boundary, not a replacement for the full school enrollment requirement.

Web3 opens first. Completing its required chapter unlocks Blockchain; completing Blockchain unlocks AI. Optional encyclopedia discoveries and supporting mini-games do not require 100 percent collection before progression. Completed worlds remain revisitable.

For the milestone, a versioned demo quest list defines its shorter chapter. Its completion must be labelled “intro chapter complete,” not completion of every full-world quest. Future content can add new quests without erasing previously earned records or claiming that an unplayed full chapter was mastered.

Core interaction loop​

  1. See the current objective and location hint.
  2. Explore a short route and inspect a relevant object or guide.
  3. Receive enough explanation to make a decision.
  4. Perform a useful action: pay, verify, repair or test.
  5. See what changed and why it succeeded or failed.
  6. Commit the checkpoint, then enable the next objective.

2.2 Controls and layout​

Landscape orientation is the proposed implementation target. Keep the approved fixed orthographic camera at roughly 35–45 degrees; avoid camera-rotation controls for the milestone.

InputActionRequired behavior
Left thumb joystickMoveStop during dialogue, modal UI, loading or saving transitions
Right contextual buttonInteractShow the nearby target and action; do not activate a distant object
Quest and encyclopedia buttonsOpen overlayPreserve the world and current objective on closing
Tap card, then destinationPlace or compare recordAlternative to dragging; selected state uses more than color
Back or pausePause or close current layerNever silently discard a confirmed purchase or completed quest

No jumping, combat, free-camera control or platforming precision is required. Test legibility, thumb reach and safe margins on a named small-screen phone. A proposed 48 dp minimum touch target is a design starting point, not a claim that the current UI meets it.

2.3 Failure and return paths​

Insufficient funds show the missing amount and a route to earn the required coins. A wrong answer reveals reasoning and allows retry. An early item remains collected. A cancelled purchase has no effect. A submitted transaction cannot be submitted twice by repeated taps. A save failure keeps the last good checkpoint and offers retry.

After closing and reopening the game, the active objective, completed actions, balances, owned items and restored scene must agree. The success animation alone is not proof that a save succeeded.

3 Game world​

3.1 World structure​

The full experience has three ordered worlds. For early production they may be three compact scenes assembled from the same island kit, not three large bespoke open worlds. Each scene needs a safe arrival point, a guide visible from the arrival route, a few readable stations and a visible restoration target.

Web3 harbor → Blockchain beacon → AI assistance center. The first world teaches use, the second teaches record integrity, and the third teaches prediction and verification. The same lantern transaction provides continuity across all three.

LocationMain landmarksRestoration outcome
Web3 harborSean’s arrival plaza, resident, merchant, workshop, exchange and repair terminalWorkshop and harbor services reopen
Blockchain beaconMatthew’s checkpoint console, damaged local display, verified reference station, stale-node stationLocal record repairs and beacon returns
AI centerLee’s example desk, practice test desk, evidence console, dispatch dockA supervised helper can assist residents

Place optional discovery objects beside required paths. Use signs, silhouettes and quest markers together; do not make a new learner hunt across the island for the tutorial. Exits explain the specific unmet main objective.

3.2 Web3 harbor​

Concept target: a short route from Sean and the resident to the merchant and dark workshop. Scene dressing is illustrative; use the smallest production layout that supports the quest.

The learner should care about the lantern because it enables restoration, not because an NPC demands that the word “NFT” be memorized. Sean links the tool, the payment and the ownership record after the player encounters them.

For W01–W04 the basic purchase itself illustrates a simple programmed condition: sufficient funds and an available item are required before the atomic exchange. The separate resident agreement in W06 adds external completion evidence and should not be confused with merely choosing “yes” in dialogue.

The resident’s 12-coin reward is a one-time quest allocation from the simulation, not a lesson claiming that all cryptocurrency is created by completing errands. There are no optional purchases before this required transaction in the core slice. Later shopping needs a tested resource-recovery route.

Example narrative and teaching beat​

Sean: “The workshop needs a lantern. Help our neighbor first, then check what the merchant is asking.”

Merchant: “Lantern #014 costs 8 Chain Coins. This transaction also needs a network fee of 1.”

Review prompt: “You have 12. How many remain after this transaction?” A wrong answer highlights the price and fee separately, then permits another try without charging the player.

After confirmation, Sean asks the learner to open the ownership record and find the lantern. At the workshop, the learner equips it without a new payment. A final new example asks whether reading the encyclopedia also costs gas. The explanation identifies which actions change the simulated network state.

A correct mathematical answer alone is insufficient: the learner must also see the item and payment change together, and distinguish the ledger record from the wallet interface.

3.3 Blockchain beacon​

Concept target: repair one damaged local station using a trusted reference. Other stations represent local simulated copies on the same phone, not connected student devices.

The beacon’s local copy is damaged. Matthew can access a trusted checkpoint and verified reference history. The learner repairs the local display and record; they do not rewrite accepted global history.

Blockchain quest sequence​

QuestAction and feedbackScope
B01 Read the familiar recordInspect the confirmed Web3 purchase, including 8-coin price, 1-coin fee and Lantern #014 transferCORE
B02 Restore the sequencePlace three subsequent display blocks after a trusted checkpoint by their previous-block referencesCORE
B03 Reject the altered copyCompare a changed 6-coin local payment with the verified 8-coin original; identify the broken referenceCORE
B04 Synchronize a stale nodeLoad verified missing blocks into a behind-but-not-tampered copyFULL
B05 Help validate a batchCheck authorization, funds, ownership and repeated transactions before a simulated network decisionFULL
B06 Compare mining and validatorsOperate a bounded nonce-search demonstration; contrast it with the main validator workflowFULL
B07 Restore the beaconRecheck the local checkpoint, restore the light and explain the repairCORE for demo; FULL synthesis later

B02 and B03 use one bounded scenario, not a general block editor. The verified reference remains read-only. Blocks may contain multiple records. Timestamps and colors may aid reading but cannot be the reason a sequence is accepted.

An out-of-date copy is not necessarily dishonest. A changed copy that breaks a trusted reference is a different fault. If no trustworthy reference exists, the correct explanation is that the original is not established, not “the player chooses which history is true.”

3.4 AI assistance center​

Concept target: a teaching desk, separate testing area and supervised dispatch station. The learning model and later assistant are distinct systems.

Lee’s helper examines fictional transaction requests. Blockchain checks whether a transaction meets network rules; the helper offers fallible advice about whether a person should review the request. A valid signature does not prove that a request is wise or honest.

AI quest sequence​

QuestAction and feedbackScope
A01 Teach with examplesRead contextual evidence; label a small example set Routine or Review; fit an explainable tiny classifierSTRETCH
A02 Test unfamiliar requestsPredict what the bot will say; run new cases; inspect mistakes; correct an example and practice againSTRETCH
A03 Make the final checkUse a reserved fresh set; explain one limitation; restore the teaching deskSTRETCH
A04 Ask and verifyBuild a request with goal, context and constraints; check an authored assistant response against the ledgerFULL
A05 Supervise dispatchInspect a proposed recipient, item and cost; approve or reject; recheck changed conditionsFULL

The recommended first implementation is a small nearest-neighbor classifier over explicitly defined features, not a neural network, cloud chatbot or financial fraud engine. It must actually depend on the learner’s labelled examples. If outputs are authored branches, label the activity an AI demonstration instead of a trained model.

Use synthetic requests only. Include a legitimate large community purchase and a suspicious small request so “large amount means bad” is visibly inadequate. An unknown case can require human review. The bot never spends without approval.

A01–A03 can form a short standalone intro chapter after the core passes. A04–A05 add modern assistant and agent concepts later; they are not required to make the first classifier lesson educationally honest.

4 Graphics and art style​

4.1 Art direction and production boundaries​

Retain the approved fixed isometric, restrained low-poly direction. Use faceted silhouettes, angular ruins, simple material surfaces, sparse painterly detail, readable long shadows and controlled glow. Water and terrain should frame paths rather than create visually busy routes.

The approved Explorer has blue hair and yellow clothing; friendly robot guides use white bodies and dark screen faces with cyan expressions. Apply the same faceted treatment to characters and environments. Do not return to the superseded rounded toy or bright voxel-diorama look.

Tunic is a camera, material-density and composition reference only. Do not copy its maps, characters, props, icons or HUD. The supplied Project Vanishing document controls the GDD’s organization, not Chainy’s camera, genre or art assets.

Minimum production kit​

Asset familyNovember candidateLater expansion
World piecesShared ground, wall, path, stair, bridge, water and foliage piecesMore regional dressing after performance checks
Interaction stationsMerchant, workshop mechanism, ledger console, beaconExchange, mining, recovery and AI dispatch stations
CharactersExisting Explorer plus one reusable robot rig with named variantsExtra resident silhouettes and reactions
InventoryRepair component, lantern, wallet icon and coin symbolBuilder Token, supplies and contract evidence icons
FeedbackObjective marker, selection outline, success/error symbol, restoration lightExtra cosmetic animation only after core acceptance

Luis provides source files, export previews, scale/origin notes, materials, animation lists and provenance. Miguel implements interactive UI. Christian validates the imported scene on Android. Final polygon counts, texture sizes, memory and package budgets follow a measured representative scene; a 4 GB RAM phone does not give the game all 4 GB.

4.2 Interface design​

The exploration HUD shows one short objective, quest/encyclopedia access, movement and interaction. Keep additional panels contextual. The wallet has Balances, Items and Activity views. Display native Chain Coin separately from Builder Token and unique equipment.

Dialogue remains over the live isometric scene: wide bottom panel, Explorer portrait on the left, speaker portrait on the right and centered choices above. Disable movement and world interaction while the overlay is active. Neutral choices must not reveal the correct answer through color before selection.

All essential feedback works with sound off and includes text or an icon as well as color. Let learners control dialogue pacing, reopen explanations and use tap-to-place instead of dragging. Keep decorative hex strings secondary to understandable sender, recipient, amount and status fields.

4.3 Storyboard and GUI development​

Three separate screen studies, not three panels shown simultaneously on a phone. The quest log separates a current goal, its next action and completed history; encyclopedia stages record learning activities rather than a mastery percentage.

PanelWhat the player seesWhat the system must do
1 Play and worldsWeb3 available; Blockchain and AI have specific unlock labelsResume the active demo checkpoint or begin the tutorial
2 ArrivalSean visible near a short path; one objectiveTeach movement and interaction without a long search
3 ExplainLive scene behind the dialogue overlayCapture only dialogue input; add the basic encyclopedia entry
4 Review purchaseItem, price, fee, total and remaining balancePermit cancel; reject invalid input before submission
5 Confirmed resultLantern ownership and activity record; new balanceCommit related state once before showing saved success

These panels define states, not eight separate scenes. Reuse overlays and the same world wherever possible. A visually finished screen without its cancel, retry, error and resume paths is not a finished interaction.

Quest log and encyclopedia​

The log presents completed, current and locked main objectives chronologically. The active entry includes its resident, place, next action and progress. Optional discoveries are visually separate from required progression.

The encyclopedia uses four records: Discovered, Explained, Practiced and Demonstrated in a new situation. None is named “mastered.” Discovery adds a basic entry immediately. Identification adds meaning and a concise contextual prompt. A wrong answer must not hide the reference needed to learn.

No quiz appears after each coin or duplicate item. Group related discoveries and let the player consult entries in the field. Each entry includes what the concept does, an island example, one common misconception and the relevant activity.

4.4 Sound design​

Use quiet island ambience, light mechanical cues and clear confirmation sounds. Give purchase review, confirmation, invalid action and restoration distinguishable sounds without alarm-heavy punishment. Music should not compete with reading.

The milestone needs a small licensed or original sound set, volume controls and a silent-play path. Voice acting, adaptive scores and unique tracks for every station are not required. Document the license and source of every shipped sound; concept images do not authorize third-party audio reuse.

5 Mechanics​

5.1 Quests and dialogue​

Quest states are Locked → Available → Active → Ready to report → Completed. A quest can skip Ready to report when its completion occurs at a station. Each transition has a prerequisite, an action, a success response and a save boundary.

SituationRequired rule
Early pickupRecord it once and count it when the relevant quest becomes active
Repeated reportReplay feedback if helpful; never repeat a one-time reward
Wrong answerShow a hint or counterexample; retain the item and allow retry
Modal openSuspend world input so one tap cannot both answer and move
Interrupted sceneReload the last committed quest state and reconstruct world changes
New contentPreserve completed objective/version records; explicitly map the next required quest

Dialogue should explain a decision in short pieces, not deliver an entire chapter before the player can act. Narrative choices may change feedback, but branching relationship systems and punishments for offending NPCs are outside scope.

5.2 Wallet and purchase​

Reference sequence: 12 coins, price 8, fee 1, total 9, remaining 3. Generated UI labels illustrate hierarchy; the rules below govern the implementation.

The wallet is an interface to account information and authorization. The ownership record belongs to the simulated ledger, not physically inside the wallet picture. An address is shareable receiving information; secret signing material is not.

Purchase states: Inspect → Review → Authorize → Pending → Confirmed or Failed. Review includes the recipient, item identifier, price, network fee and total. Cancelling before submission changes nothing. Confirmation changes balances, item ownership, activity and quest state atomically and once. Equipping an owned lantern does not submit another payment.

The milestone uses toy authorization checks clearly labelled a simulation. It does not need real seed generation, cryptographic signing, a public node or a blockchain SDK. If production later implements real hashes, use full hashes internally while shortening only their display. [S3–S5]

<!-- page -->

5.3 Economy and ownership rules​

Chain Coin is the fictional native currency used for the illustrated network fee. Builder Token is a fungible asset issued within that simulated network. Lantern #014 is a unique item whose token ownership is recorded; a separate receipt describes a transaction. Not every stackable supply needs an NFT. NFTs do not guarantee investment value or transfer every creative right. [S3, S4, S6]

For the first purchase, the learner starts with 12 Chain Coins and spends exactly 9 including the fee. Failed review checks charge nothing. Later lessons may illustrate an executed failed transaction consuming a fee, but must distinguish that from a pre-submission rejection. Movement, reading, viewing balances and ordinary equipment use are free. [S4]

The full exchange uses a displayed fixed teaching quote, with input, output, fee and post-trade balances. Show that swapping assets differs from buying a tool. Prevent a required quest from becoming unwinnable because all fee currency was exchanged. No speculative prices, scarcity manipulation or real-money purchase is needed.

Smart contract interaction​

A full repair agreement exposes the condition, trigger and result: “Required supplies received AND valid workshop completion evidence submitted AND reward not already claimed.” The player invokes the claim. A completion terminal supplies the in-game evidence; the program does not magically observe a real bridge or workshop.

An unmet condition explains what is missing. The condition is rechecked at commit time. A repeated claim returns Already claimed without another reward. A purchase can use a simpler conditional program before this broader quest is built. This is a teaching simulation, not executable Solidity or a deployed contract. [S5]

Seed phrase recovery​

In the full version, Sean helps secure a game-only backup before a later recovery exercise. Never scatter secret words as public treasure or ask the learner to enter a real wallet secret. Use an unmistakable “Practice backup — not a real wallet” screen and bounded fictional symbols or words.

Recovery restores access to the same simulated account and its existing ownership records; it does not mint a second lantern or reset balances. The fictional backup is unrelated to the student voucher, learner identity, teacher PIN or actual device save. A lost phone’s unsynced progress cannot be recovered by this lesson mechanic.

The first milestone can explain backup safety through dialogue, but that does not count as an implemented hands-on seed-recovery mechanic.

5.4 Blockchain inspection and repair​

Reference interaction: inspect a trusted checkpoint, link the display blocks and reject changed data. The plate shows two placement slots for readability; the proposed core scenario uses three subsequent blocks. A/B/C are symbolic reference labels, not cryptographic hashes.

The core repair uses a known starting checkpoint and three subsequent display blocks. Each block exposes its identifier, previous reference and a concise record summary. One record refers to the actual lantern purchase. One block may contain several transactions, so the interface must not teach a universal one-transaction-per-block rule.

The learner selects a block, inspects its evidence and places it. A mismatch explains which reference failed. In the tamper case, the damaged display changes the price from 8 to 6; the trusted record still says 8. Restore from the verified source rather than asking the learner to arbitrarily edit the accepted payment.

The full version adds stale-node synchronization and candidate-transaction checks for authorization, balance, ownership and replay. A network decision is simulated separately; one player clicking Approve is not real consensus. Nodes and validators are related roles, not synonyms. [S7, S8]

The mining station is a separate proof-of-work comparison: vary a nonce, search for a hash meeting a toy target and show that verification is quicker than the search. Bound the demonstration and CPU work. Do not turn mining into hitting a rock or imply that the main proof-of-stake network also mines its blocks. [S8, S9]

The beacon’s restoration condition is a valid local reconstruction against the scenario’s trusted reference. It is not a claim that every real node must always be online and identical at the same instant.

<!-- page -->

5.5 AI teaching and testing​

Reference interaction: separate labelled examples from new test cases, show advisory predictions and preserve human review. The left panel shows labels already selected; the playable version must offer both choices first. The panels are separate screens, not simultaneous phone UI. Artwork does not establish model accuracy.

Use a small, curated synthetic dataset. Proposed features are known recipient, amount relative to usual activity and permission type. Each card also has story evidence so the learner is not labelling blindly. Labels mean Routine and Needs review, not guaranteed safe and criminal.

A candidate three-nearest-neighbor model compares normalized features and takes a label vote. Define ties, missing features and very unfamiliar cases as Review. The exact feature encoding and distance rule need a small prototype; the acceptance requirement is that changing relevant training labels or examples can change predictions on held-out requests. Do not display a vote fraction as a calibrated confidence percentage.

A proposed teaching set contains six examples, a separate four-case practice set and a reserved four-case final set. These counts are content-production starting points, not statistical validation. Practice feedback can guide corrections; final-set labels must not influence training before the recorded first final attempt. Replays become practice and must not be described as fresh independent tests. [S10]

Include counterexamples with sufficient context: a large approved workshop purchase, and a small request for inappropriate permission. A fresh final scenario should test the learner’s reasoning, not whether the bot can be forced to score perfectly.

For the later assistant activity, bundle authored responses and clearly label them simulated. A learner supplies goal, context and constraints, then opens records to verify a plausible answer. Prompt context is not retraining, and an AI text token is not a blockchain asset token. Later dispatch is a bounded scripted agent workflow: observe, propose, ask permission, act and inspect. It is not evidence that ordinary pathfinding has learned a model. [S11]

5.8 Local state and implementation boundaries​

Only one activity owns gameplay input at a time: exploration, dialogue, purchase, record repair or AI exercise. Opening a modal releases movement. A pending commit blocks repeated confirmation; closing a panel does not cancel a transaction already committed.

Reuse the existing controller and dialogue. Reconcile new quest, simulation and save responsibilities with Christian’s actual Game Manager, Progress Manager, Database Manager, event and scene interfaces. Do not begin with a replacement framework or new service platform.

SQLite remains the local store. Minimum persistent gameplay state includes profile ID, build/content/schema versions, active/completed quests, discovered entries, inventory and balances, ledger state, world restoration flags and relevant activity checkpoints. These are proposed record needs, not verified table definitions.

For a purchase or reward, balance, ownership, quest and ledger changes must succeed together. Use a stable action identifier to make duplicate taps and repeated resume handling harmless. Confirm “Saved” only after the database confirms success. Test interrupted commits, no-space failures, force-close and relaunch with synthetic profiles.

Data-heavy systems are deferred, but postponing save design until after all quests would be a delivery risk. The smallest reliable save is part of the first complete loop.

5.9 Later teacher and distribution service​

The full product retains Teacher/Student entry, developer-issued teacher vouchers, one online teacher activation, offline student pairing, a protected dashboard and local collection for approximately 20–40 personal student phones. Teachers later upload individual learner-coded results; names and the identity mapping remain with the teacher.

Separate three payloads: logical learning records, compatible content packages and signed Android app updates. They require different validation, retry and recovery rules. A teacher dashboard shows last received data, not live progress while phones are disconnected. Student devices are not nodes in the fictional blockchain lesson.

Nearby Connections or a local Wi-Fi session are candidates requiring device prototypes. No promise is made that 40 phones connect simultaneously, that updates are silent, or that SQLite itself provides synchronization. These capabilities are outside the November gameplay commitment. [S12, S13]

Do not collect real learner records in an improvised developer service. School authorization, receiving account, purpose, retention and deletion rules remain prerequisites. Demo tests use synthetic codes. A data-collection or school-evaluation obligation must be resolved with the adviser, not silently removed because services are deferred.
